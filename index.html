Overview

A tool built for everyone,
tested by no one

The Sustainable Events Carbon Emissions Calculator helps NYC event organizers estimate the carbon footprint of their events across five categories: Venue and Materials, Energy, Transportation, Food and Drink, and Waste. It was built through a collaboration between the NYC Mayor's Office of Operations, the NYC Department of Sanitation, and the NYC Town+Gown program.

The calculator had a lot going for it, despite it being a spreadsheet. Organized tabs, auto-calculating formulas, a benchmark comparison from a dataset of 954 events, and a summary page that pulled everything together. The one thing it was missing was a real user's perspective in trying to use it.

Our team of four from Pratt Institute's DX Center was brought in to run user testing and figure out what was working and what wasn't.

My Role

I ran two unmoderated testing sessions on my own and took notes for two additional moderated sessions. I spent a lot of time observing people open this calculator for the first time and seeing exactly where things went wrong.

That experience shaped my main contribution: designing and building the landing page, which became Recommendation 1.

Research and Methods

Getting eight strangers to open
a spreadsheet on Zoom

We recruited eight participants through DSCOUT Private Panels and local event networks. All of them had organized events in New York City before, ranging from university wide events to city events and concerts. Most of these event planners had little to no background in sustainability or carbon tracking, which allowed us to test the calculator with varying expertise and knowledge in this area and gave us valuable insights.

Each session ran about 30 minutes over Zoom where participants talked through what they were seeing and doing as they went through the tool. We kept sessions to one interviewer and one note taker to stay consistent and avoid influencing how people responded and reacted.

Testing a spreadsheet is also not something most usability research is set up for. There is no user flow to map nor was there a clickable prototype to hand someone. We had to pay extra attention to small things, like where someone paused, what they skipped, and what made them scroll back and forth, and figure out how to turn those moments into useful findings.

What We Found

The same thing kept
happening in every session

Almost every session started the same way. A participant would open the spreadsheet, land on the Summary tab, look around for a few seconds, and say some version of the same thing.

The calculator wasn't broken. It just didn't explain itself. There was no introduction, no guidance on where to start, and no indication of which tabs were relevant for different types of events. A club event organizer and a large concert producer opened the same spreadsheet with no way of knowing their experience should look completely different.

The confusion came up in four consistent areas across all eight sessions.

Recommendations

Four problems,
four recommendations

Based on the testing, we put together four recommendations. Each one addressed a different part of the problem. And they all connected. A user who didn't understand the tool wouldn't open the tabs. A user who didn't know which tabs to open wouldn't fill in the data. A user who didn't fill in the data wouldn't get to the results. And a user who got to the results had no way to make sense of them.

That meant the order mattered. Recommendation 1 had to come first because everything else depended on it.

Recommendation 01 — My Work Angela Molina

The landing page:
where I started

After spending time in testing sessions and watching people open the spreadsheet and immediately feel lost, it was only natural to give the user context as to what they were looking at. Which prompted me to add a landing page to the tool to properly introduce itself.

All the other recommendations assumed users had already decided to engage with the calculator. But if someone didn't know what they were looking at in the first thirty seconds, they weren't going to get far enough to benefit from any of those changes.

I organized the landing page around six sections, each one based on a question that came up directly in testing.

Before

Users landed here with no introduction, no guidance, and no idea where to start.

After

A dedicated first tab that answers every question a first-time user would have before they touch any data.

SECTION 01
What is this calculator?
A short description of the tool, what it measures, and who it is for. This didn't exist anywhere in the original workbook.
SECTION 02
How do I use it?
A step-by-step guide through the tab order, where to enter data, and what the Summary tab shows at the end.
SECTION 03
What do I actually need to fill in?
An explanation of where to input data in the tabs. This is where introducing the orange border from Recommendation 3 is explained.
SECTION 04
Which tabs do I need?
A short guide organized by event type. A community fair organizer can immediately see which tabs apply to them and which ones to skip.
SECTION 05
Tab directory
A table listing every tab, what it covers, and when to use it.
SECTION 06
Understanding your results
A plain-language explanation of tCO2e and how the benchmark comparison works, so users know what their number means before they get to the Summary tab.

Recommendation 02 Francois Yap

Giving each tab
a little more context

Once a user got past the landing page, the next issue was that each tab dropped them straight into a data table with no explanation of what it was asking or why. Participants in the Transportation tab said they didn't know what the labels meant. In the Waste tab, one participant mentioned they track waste in bags, not by weight, and would have prepared differently if they had known what the calculator needed.

The recommendation was to add a short guidance section at the top of each tab before the data table starts. It would explain what the tab covers, what information to have ready, and what to skip if something doesn't apply. Each tab would also show a running emissions subtotal so users could see the impact of what they were entering without going back to the Summary tab each time.

Venue and Material tab with plain-language guidance at the top and a running emissions subtotal at the bottom

Recommendation 03 Harleen Kaur

Making the inputs
easier to read

The visual design of the calculator was also causing confusion. The red border around the input column, which was meant to show users where to type, was being read as an error. The color coding on the emissions column was inconsistent. And columns like EF (kg CO2e/unit) and Default added a lot of information that most users didn't need and couldn't interpret.

The recommendation was to remove the Default and EF columns from the main view and move them to the reference tab for anyone who wants to see the methodology. The input border would switch to a lighter orange accent that signals "enter here" without looking like something is wrong. Hover notes on each input cell would show a suggested estimate based on event type and a short description of what the field is asking for.

Simplified tab with orange input border and hover notes showing suggested estimates by event type

Recommendation 04 Sharron Lou

Making the results
mean something

After going through the whole calculator, users arrived at the Summary tab and saw their total emissions number. Most of them had no idea what to do with it. There was no explanation of what tCO2e means, the benchmark comparison was easy to miss, and there was nothing telling them what to do next.

The recommendation was to add a dedicated Emissions Report tab with five sections: a summary of key metrics with benchmark indicators, a plain-language explanation of tCO2e and how the result compares to similar events worldwide, a breakdown of emissions by category, a prioritized list of next steps ranked by impact, and a glossary of key terms. All figures update automatically when inputs change, and the report is designed to be shareable with sponsors, venues, or city agencies.

The Emissions Report tab — emissions by category, prioritized next steps, and a glossary of key terms

Beyond the Brief

Something we built
that nobody asked for

We decided to produce a full web-based version of the calculator in Figma. It wasn't part of the deliverables, but it showed what the tool could look like as a proper digital product, with tab navigation, sliders for transportation mode share, a shareable report, and hover notes explaining technical terms in plain language.

It ended up being one of the most memorable parts of our client presentation. This helped us showcase to the client that the recommendations weren't just fixes for the spreadsheet, but a direction the tool could grow toward.

Challenges

A few things that
made this harder

📊
Testing a spreadsheet is not typical
Most usability research is set up for websites and apps. There is no prototype to click through with a spreadsheet. We had to figure out how to observe and interpret small moments, like a pause or a scroll, and turn them into recommendations for a tool that had to be improved on its existing format. This was not an easy task considering we ourselves were just as confused when we first saw what we had to work with. It took a lot of time understanding what the tool was for us to understand what we should be asking during the test and how to drive the conversation to get the best data and feedback from participants.
🤝
Navigating the client relationship
The Town+Gown team was careful about their contacts, which was understandable since participants were from their professional network and held high positions in the industry. There was a moment where stakeholders wanted to be involved in sessions in a way the method didn't allow for, which could have created biased data. We had to build trust with the client to keep sessions to two people and protect the integrity of the research.
🚀
The web prototype was out of scope
We decided to build a full Figma version of the calculator as a digital product despite the limited time we had. It wasn't asked for, but it gave the client a concrete sense of where the tool could go and what the recommendations could look like in a different format.

Reflection

Users didn't need the calculator to be simpler. They needed it to talk to them first.

A tool can be well-built and genuinely useful and still lose people in the first thirty seconds because it assumes too much about what they already know. Context is part of the design.

Building the landing page showed me how much a single clear introduction can do. It's not an animated tutorial or an onboarding flow. It's just a page that says: here's what this is, here's how it works, here's where you start. For event organizers trying to track sustainability without a background in carbon accounting, that was what they needed.

Seeing how the four recommendations fit together also taught me something useful. Each one only worked if the one before it was already in place. Figuring out what needs to be true before anything else can work, and starting there, is a good way to think about any design problem.
